In an emergency, people do not always think clearly, and phones may not work. A family emergency plan means everyone already knows what to do, where to go and who to contact. It takes about an hour to make and can save a lot of worry.

You do not need special forms. A sheet of paper per person works well. Keep it simple enough that a child could follow it.

## Step 1: think about your risks

Think about what is most likely where you live. The UK government's Be Ready site lists common hazards, such as:

- flooding
- storms
- cold weather, snow and ice
- heatwaves
- power cuts
- water supply interruptions
- fires in the home

Check your [flood risk on GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/check-flooding). If you live near a river or the coast, flooding should be central to your plan.

## Step 2: agree how you will stay in touch

- **Write down key numbers** on paper for each person: family members, a trusted neighbour, school, work, your GP and your insurer.
- **Choose an out-of-area contact**: a friend or relative who lives elsewhere. If local phone lines are busy, it can be easier to reach someone further away, and everyone can check in with them.
- **Agree to text rather than call** if networks are busy. Texts often get through when calls do not.
- Make sure everyone's phone can receive [emergency alerts](https://www.gov.uk/alerts).

## Step 3: choose meeting places

Agree two places:

1. **Near home:** somewhere just outside, such as a neighbour's front gate or the end of the road, in case you have to leave quickly (for example, a fire).
2. **Further away:** somewhere in your area, such as a relative's house, a library or a place of worship, in case you cannot get home or your area is cut off.

Make sure everyone knows how to get to both.

## Step 4: decide who does what

Agree who is responsible for:

- collecting children from school or nursery, and a backup if that person cannot get there
- checking on older relatives or neighbours who need help
- taking the grab bag and pets
- turning off gas, electricity and water if advised

Check your children's school or nursery emergency arrangements and make sure they have up-to-date contact details and a list of who may collect your children.

## Step 5: plan for people who need extra help

Think about anyone who:

- has a disability or a long-term health condition
- relies on medicines or medical equipment
- does not speak English well
- has young children or is pregnant

Write down their needs, their medicines and who supports them. Consider joining the Priority Services Register if they are eligible.

## Step 6: plan for pets

Decide where pets could go if you had to leave, since many emergency shelters cannot take them. Keep a carrier, lead and a few days' food ready.

## Step 7: plan your home

Make sure the adults in your household know:

- where the gas meter, electricity fuse box and water stopcock are
- how to turn them off, and when it is safe to do so
- where your grab bag, torches and first aid kit are kept

## Step 8: write it down and share it

Put the plan on a single page. Include:

- [ ] names and dates of birth of everyone in the household
- [ ] key phone numbers, including an out-of-area contact
- [ ] your two meeting places
- [ ] who collects whom
- [ ] medical needs and medicines
- [ ] pet arrangements
- [ ] where the stopcock, fuse box and gas meter are
- [ ] where your grab bag and important documents are kept

Give everyone a copy. Keep one in your grab bag, one by the phone or on the fridge, and one with a trusted person outside your home.

## Step 9: practise

A plan you have never tried is easy to forget. Every so often:

- walk to your meeting places with children
- check everyone can find the torch and grab bag in the dark
- test that everyone knows how to reach the out-of-area contact

## Step 10: keep it up to date

Review your plan every 6 months, or when something changes: a new home, school, job, baby or pet. Our [Home Preparedness Score](/tools/preparedness-score/) is a quick way to check how ready your household is.

## Talking to children about it

Keep it calm and practical. Explain that emergencies are rare, that the plan is like a fire drill, and that it means everyone will know what to do. Give children a simple job, such as checking the torch or packing their comfort toy, so they feel involved.
